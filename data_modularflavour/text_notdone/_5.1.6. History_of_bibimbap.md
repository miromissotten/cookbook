#### Origin
Shortly said, the origin of bibimbap is unknown. But that makes it all the more fun to speculate on.

The 2 versions I like the most:

- Some scholars assert that bibimbap originates from the traditional practice of mixing all the food offerings made at a _jesa_ (ancestral rite) in a bowl before partaking of it.9(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibimbap#cite_note-9) People could have started mixing [_bap_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bap_\(rice_dish\) "Bap (rice dish)") (rice) with _[banchan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banchan "Banchan")_ (side dishes) after the outdoor _[jesa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesa "Jesa")_ (rites), such as _[sansinje](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sansin "Sansin")_ (rite for mountain gods) or _[dongsinje](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dongsin "Dongsin")_ (rite for village gods), where they needed to "eat with the god" but did not have as many cooking pots and items of crockery on hand as they would normally have at home.10(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibimbap#cite_note-Hwang_1995-10)
- Ordinary people ate bibimbap on the eve of the [lunar new year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_New_Year "Korean New Year"). The people at that time felt that they had to get rid of all the leftover side dishes before the new year. The solution to this problem was to put all the leftovers in a bowl of rice and mix them together.14(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibimbap#cite_note-14) Farmers ate bibimbap during farming season as it was the easiest way to make food for a large number of people.15(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibimbap#cite_note-15) Bibimbap came from early rural Koreans taking leftover vegetables, sometimes having meat, with rice and mixing them in a [bowl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowl "Bowl"). This was cheap and did not require all of the time and space of a traditional meal.11(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibimbap#cite_note-:1-11)

The first known recipe for bibimbap — called ‘bubimbap’ at the time — was recorded at some point in the later part of the same century, in a cookbook called Siuijeonseo, a cooking book written in the late 19th century.

#### Versions

- **Dolsot bibimbap (돌솥비빔밥)** — Served in a hot stone bowl; the rice develops a crispy, toasted crust (_nurungji_) at the bottom.
	- Hot stone pot bibimbap (_dolsot-bibimbap_, 돌솥 비빔밥)27(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibimbap#cite_note-standardized-27) is a variation of bibimbap served in a very hot _[dolsot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolsot "Dolsot")_ (stone pot) in which a raw [egg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egg_\(food\) "Egg (food)") is cooked against the sides of the bowl. The bowl is so hot that anything that touches it sizzles for minutes. Before the rice is placed in the bowl, the bottom of the bowl is coated with [sesame oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sesame_oil "Sesame oil"), making the layer of the rice touching the bowl cook to a crisp, golden brown known as _[nurungji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nurungji "Nurungji")_ (누릉지). This variation of bibimbap is typically served to order, with the egg and other ingredients mixed in the pot just before consumption.
	- Stone pots, especially vessels made of _gopdol_ (soapstone), had long been used in Korean cuisine before the modern form of _dolsot bibimbap_ appeared. Historical sources describe stone pots as suitable for cooking rice and porridge, and they were also used for preparing tea and medicinal decoctions.28(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibimbap#cite_note-28)
	- The modern restaurant dish known as _dolsot bibimbap_ is commonly attributed to Jungang Hoegwan, a bibimbap restaurant located near the Jeonju Post Office. According to the Hansik Culture Dictionary, its owner Namgung Seong developed a stone bowl in 1969 in order to keep bibimbap warm for a longer time. The restaurant then sold bibimbap in these stone bowls under the registered name "Jeonju gopdol bibimbap", and the dish later spread to Seoul after attracting attention at a folk food event organized by Shinsegae Department Store.29(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibimbap#cite_note-29)
	- In Japan it is called by Ishiyaki bibimbap. It was popularized in Japan through Korean restaurants in Tsuruhashi, Osaka, a district known for Korean cuisine. One commonly cited example is Hakuundai (白雲台), a long-established yakiniku restaurant founded in Tsuruhashi in 1975. Kansai TV reported that Hakuundai is regarded as a restaurant that helped spread ishiyaki bibimbap in Japan. According to the restaurant's owner, Yong-o Oh, when Hakuundai began serving the dish in the late 1980s, stone bowls were not yet available in Japan, and his father, who had come from Korea, brought stone pots from Korea because he believed the dish would become popular in Japan.30(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibimbap#cite_note-30)
	- In a 2022 interview, Oh similarly recalled that, at a time when stone bowls for the dish were unavailable in Japan, he and his brother were taken to Korea to purchase them, importing around 300 bowls. He also stated that Hakuundai may have been the first restaurant in Japan to serve ishiyaki bibimbap, while noting that he had no documentary proof for this claim.31(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibimbap#cite_note-31)
- **Yukhoe bibimbap (육회비빔밥)** — Topped with Korean-style raw beef (_yukhoe_), often with egg yolk and sesame oil.
	- _Yakcho-bibimbap_ (약초비빔밥) is from [Jecheon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jecheon "Jecheon"). Yak (약) is a historical term for medicinal. Jecheon is a great place for [medicinal herbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicinal_herbs "Medicinal herbs") to grow. People could get a thicker root and more medicinal herb than in other areas. The combination of the medicinal herbs and popular bibimbap made it one of the most popular foods in Jecheon.32(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibimbap#cite_note-32)
- **Jeonju bibimbap (전주비빔밥)** — The famous regional style from Jeonju, typically featuring many seasoned vegetables, beef, egg, and _gochujang_.
	- **Jeonju bibimbap** ([Korean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_language "Korean language"): 전주비빔밥) along with kongnamul-gukbap (bean sprout and rice soup) are signature dishes of Jeonju.21(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibimbap#cite_note-:2-21) Jeonju bibimbap is usually topped with soy bean sprouts, hwangpo-muk, gochujang, jeopjang, and seasoned raw beef and served with kongnamul-gukbap. The rice of Jeonju bibimbap is specially prepared by being cooked in beef shank broth for flavor and finished with sesame oil for flavor and nutrients.21(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibimbap#cite_note-:2-21) Jeonju also holds a [Jeonju Bibimbap Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeonju_Bibimbap_Festival "Jeonju Bibimbap Festival") every year.22(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibimbap#cite_note-22)
- **Tongyeong bibimbap (통영비빔밥)** — A coastal variation traditionally incorporating a wider range of seafood and local vegetables.
- **Jinju bibimbap (진주비빔밥)** — A regional style from Jinju, traditionally served with various vegetables and beef, sometimes alongside a clear soup.
	- **Jinju bibimbap** ([Korean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_language "Korean language"): 진주비빔밥) is a regional variety of _bibimbap_ associated with Jinju, South Gyeongsang Province. It is typically made by mixing rice with assorted seasoned vegetables, beef _yukhoe_, broth known as _potangguk_ or _botangguk_, and red-pepper paste seasoning.23(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibimbap#cite_note-23)
	- Distinctive features of Jinju bibimbap include finely seasoned vegetables, the addition of _botangguk_, beef _yukhoe_, and a special red-pepper paste known as _yeot-gojang_. The dish has also been called _hwaban_, meaning "flower bowl", because of the colorful arrangement of vegetables and other toppings over white rice.25(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibimbap#cite_note-25)26(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibimbap#cite_note-26)
- **Hoedeopbap (회덮밥)** — A seafood version, using raw fish and vegetables over rice, usually with a spicy _cho-gochujang_ sauce.
	- _[Hoedeopbap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoedeopbap "Hoedeopbap")_ (회덮밥) is a bibimbap with a variety of raw seafood, such as [olive flounder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olive_flounder "Olive flounder"), [salmon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salmon_as_food "Salmon as food"), [tuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuna_as_food "Tuna as food") or sometimes [octopus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octopus_as_food "Octopus as food"), but each bowl of rice usually contains only one variety of seafood. The term _[hoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoe_\(dish\) "Hoe (dish)")_ in the word means raw fish. The dish is popular along the coasts of Korea, where fish are abundant.33(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibimbap#cite_note-33)

#### Traditions

Barley tea is poured into the hot stone bowl after dolsot bibimbap**, it’s called **sungnyung (숭늉)**.

It’s made by pouring hot water (sometimes barley or corn tea) over the **crispy scorched rice—nurungji (누룽지)** left at the bottom of the dolsot. You let it soften and then drink/eat the warm, lightly toasted rice infusion at the end of the meal.

So:
- **Dolsot** = stone pot
- **Nurungji** = crispy scorched rice
- **Sungnyung** = hot-water/tea infusion made from that rice crust
