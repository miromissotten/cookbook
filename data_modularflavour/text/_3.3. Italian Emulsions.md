### What Is an Emulsion?
An emulsion is a mix between two liquids that don’t naturally mix. Left alone, they will separate. In this case fat and water.

Why do they auto-separate? Water molecules are polar, fat molecules are nonpolar. Polar substances can be compared to magnets (though weak, but still magnets), apolar substances can be compared to wood dust. If you put magnets in wood dust, when you stir the mixture, the magnets will stick together.

Now, we don't want them to auto-separate, and that can be solved with by creating an emulsion. An emulsion forces them into suspension of one another: microscopic droplets of one should become dispersed throughout the other. There's multiple techniques in how to create an emulsion between auto-separating substances, and we'll go through some of the most prominent ones in Italian pasta dishes.

In Italian pasta dishes, emulsions are built from:

- **Fat:** could be olive oil, guanciale fat, butter, ...
- **Water:** Pasta cooking water
- **Emulsifier:** Egg proteins, cheese proteins, starch
- **Mechanical Energy:** Stirring, tossing, whisking, ...

The 3 methods which will be mentioned, are the usage of egg, usage of cheese, and usage of starch.

- Egg yolks contain a protein called lecithin.
	- Since lecithin has a hydrophilic ("water-loving") and hydrophobic ("water-hating" aka "fat-loving") side, it can form a stable bond between water and fat to create a stable emulsion. It prevents the water and fat to group together with their own kind.
- Cheese contains a protein called casein, a milk protein.
	- Casein is a longer molecular chain, and thus there are multiple hydrophilic and hydrophobic areas. At those areas, a weak bond can be formed with water or fat.
- Starch increases the viscosity of water when dissolved.
	- This makes the droplets inside of the water to move more slowly. Think of it like you moving fast through air, but more slowly through water. the same is true, but then between water and "viscous water". *(Technically speaking, starch doesn't really create an emulsion, but only a temporary suspension. I'll consider it an emulsifier anyway for ease of description.)*

### Concepts in practice

#### Carbonara
- **Fat:** guanciale fat (or olive oil for a vegetarian version)
- **Emulsifier:** Egg+cheese+starch

Carbonara is the most stable of the three dishes. It's the easiest dish of the 3 to get a sauce, but the easiest one to mess up as well in creation.
Firstly, it uses egg, which is the most stable emulsion discussed. That on its own could already work, using a raw egg to bond fat and pasta water. However, the emulsion can be improved significantly by partially coagulating the egg proteins. Gentle heat partially coagulates the proteins: up to about 70°C, from 75°C onwards, the eggs start to solidify and become a scrambled egg, which should be avoided. When the eggs are partially coagulated, their viscosity increases like starch increase the viscosity of water.
As a third step, we also add cheese. The fourth step is adding some starch which clings to the pasta

#### Cacio e Pepe
- **Fat phase:** Fat from Pecorino Romano
- **Emulsifier:** cheese+starch

Cacio e pepe is less stable than carbonara because it doesn't use egg. It's emulsion components are the cheese and the starch. 
Pecorino Romano is an aged, low-moisture cheese. Its proteins can easily clump, tighten, and separate into oil and solids if overheated. This makes cacio e pepe very temperature-sensitive and dependent on the starch concentration and mechanical force used. Also take into account, Cacio e pepe is a cheese emulsion, not melted cheese. If the cheese melts into strings, the emulsion has failed.
There's many ways this dish can fail, but it's perfect when it works out. 

#### Aglio e Olio: A Pure Mechanical Emulsion
- **Fat phase:** Olive oil
- **Emulsifier:** starch

This is the most minimal and most fragile of the three. There is no egg and no cheese protein; only starch holds it together unstably, though slowly and steadily, it loses it's emulsion properties.

Risotto uses the same type of emulsion, but using the big amount of starch which leaks from the risotto rice. Fettucini Alfredo uses also the same technique but using butter as a fat.
