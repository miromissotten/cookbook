#### naming
Sweet potatoes are called sweet potatoes purely by visual ressemblence. They are actually completely different families. Sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas) vs regular potato (Solanum tuberosum). Discovery people didn't have all the knowledge at their fingertips as we do today, which makes for these funny misnamers.
Later, the prefix "sweet" was added, because they contain more natural sugars than regular potatoes, and it distinguishes them.

#### note on sugars
Important note for this page: when talking about sugars, I mean chemical sugars, not "supermarket sugars". 

- Glucose is the most simple sugar you can imagine. It is the sugar we typically associate with sweets
- Maltose is a chain of 2 glucose molecules. I also tastes very sweet, but a little less sweet.
- Starch is a chain of 100+/1000+ glucose molecules. It does not taste sweet on its own.

Starch are polysaccharides (many little glucose molecules), which is why I'll call them sugars, but in a supermarket, you won't see a label "100% sugar" on a bag of corn flour. However, your digestive system will break down most starch to maltose and then glucose. So in the end, it's all the same really (shortcut, yes).

#### the composition
What makes them interesting to me, is their molecular composition.  Sweet potato contains a lot of starch and starch-degrading enzymes. Of this starch, it's mainly amylopectin (75%) and amylose (25%). Of the starch-gedrading enzymes, it's mainly α-amylase and β-amylase. 

- α-amylase can be compared to a scissor which only cuts in the middle of chains, splitting long chains into 2 or more half-as-long chains.
- β-amylase can be compared to a scissor which cuts only at the ends, cutting one by one the chain up into maltose molecules.

β-amylase is the one that makes it taste sweet, but α-amylase significantly speeds up the process by creating more ends to cut from.

#### the reaction
The reaction between the starch and starch-degrading molecules only starts when the starch is reachable for the amylase (as in: it gelatinises and opens up) and when the amylase gets enough heat energy to be active. If it would always work, every sweet potato would be a sugar bomb.

When you put the starch and starch degrading enzymes together at specific temperatures, the following happens (note that the specific temperatures depend per type of sweet potato):

| temperature | α-amylase                  | β-amylase                  | Starch                                                      |
| ----------- | -------------------------- | -------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| **40–50°C** | Not active                 | Not active                 | remains relatively intact due to limited reaction potential |
| **50–60°C** | Operates near its peak     | Slowly becomes more active | starts to gelatinise but remains relatively intact          |
| **60–70°C** | Slowly becomes less active | Operates near its peak     | becomes gelatinous and easier for amylase to break down     |
| **70–80°C** | Barely active              | Activity rapidly declines  | reaches its gelatinisation peak                             |
| **80+°C**   | Inactive                   | Inactive                   | gelatinisation is saturated                                 |







This is also one of the reasons why the saying exists
>if it grows in the ground, boil it from room temperature.

If you wouldn't heat it from room temperature to boiling with the potato in it, it would skip steps (well not really because the potato still has to heat up, but it'll be much quicker through each step at least).
