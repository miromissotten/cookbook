There are many ways in which soups can be categorised.

- Liquids with suspended vegetables/meat, I won't talk about as I don't like the exhausting concentration while cutting the food. About halfway, I'm bored of it and it becomes a sad mixture of sizes after all.
- Liquids where all solids were extracted from the end result, are broths/stocks/bouillon. One of those words is the meat-y version, the others are not, but I can't for the life of me keep it in my head, so I'll use the three of them interchangeably throughout the chapter (and probably other parts of the book). These are usually *(and I say usually for very good reasons which will become apparent later)* used as a light base to float the main ingredient in. They're usually not the star of the show. The basic versions described are the liquid for a [[_3.1.2. Quick Japanese Broth|quick japanese-style broth]], a [[_3.1.3.0. Ramen_Bouillon|deep ramen bouillon]], and a more classical [[_3.1.1. French Vegetable Bouillon|french vegetable bouillon]]. For the ramen bouillon, more details on how to make the flavour, are listed as well.
- Liquids with blended vegetables on the other hand, are usually used as the star of the show, and are heavier in both presence and feeling in the dish. A few favourites of mine are [[_3.1.4. Soup Potato & Leek|potato & leek soup]], [[_3.1.7. Soup Carrot & Miso|miso & carrot soup]], and [[_3.1.8. Kimchi-guk|kimchi soup]]
- Liquids where you have more solids than liquid, are stews. There aren't many discussed here as I haven't yet deepdived into that world. The only ones discussed here are [[_3.1.5. Soup Sundubu Jjigae|sundubu jjigae]] and a [[_3.1.6. Soup Tom Kha Vegetarian|vegetarian tom kha]].
